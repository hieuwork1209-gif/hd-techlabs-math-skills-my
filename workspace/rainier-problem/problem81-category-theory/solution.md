## Steps

Step 1: Classify one invertible Beck distributive law
Let
$$
D=k[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2),
$$
and let $T_i=D\otimes_k-$ be four labeled copies of the corresponding monad on $\operatorname{Vect}_k$.
For $i<j$, an invertible Beck distributive law
$$
\lambda_{ji}:T_jT_i\Rightarrow T_iT_j
$$
is determined by its value on $D_j\otimes D_i$. Write $x=\varepsilon_i$ and $y=\varepsilon_j$. The unit axioms force
$$
\lambda(1\otimes1)=1\otimes1,
\qquad
\lambda(1\otimes x)=x\otimes1,
\qquad
\lambda(y\otimes1)=1\otimes y.
$$
Write
$$
\lambda(y\otimes x)=a+b x+c y+dxy.
$$
The two multiplication axioms, applied to $y\otimes x\otimes x$ and $y\otimes y\otimes x$, give
$$
b=c=0,
\qquad
a(1+d)=0.
$$
The matrix of $\lambda$ in the basis $1,x,y,xy$ has determinant $d$, so invertibility is equivalent to $d\ne0$. Thus every invertible Beck law has the form
$$
\lambda(y\otimes x)=a+dxy,
\qquad
a(1+d)=0,
\qquad d\in k^\times.
$$
The associated twisted tensor-product algebra has generators $x,y$ and relations
$$
x^2=y^2=0,
\qquad
yx=a+dxy.
$$

Step 2: Detect pairwise simplicity
If $a=0$, the span of $x$ and $xy$ is a nonzero proper two-sided ideal, so the pair algebra is not simple.

If $a\ne0$, the Beck equation forces $d=-1$, so
$$
yx+xy=a.
$$
The assignment
$$
x\mapsto E_{12},
\qquad
y\mapsto aE_{21}
$$
gives an isomorphism with $M_2(k)$. Hence a pairwise composite monad is simple exactly when its scalar parameter $a_{ji}$ is nonzero.

There are six pairs among four labels, so the first extremal value is at most $6$.

Step 3: Impose all Yang-Baxter coherences
For every triple $i<j<\ell$, evaluating the Yang-Baxter equation on
$$
\varepsilon_\ell\otimes\varepsilon_j\otimes\varepsilon_i
$$
gives the same three relations as in the three-monad case:
$$
a_{\ell j}(1-d_{ji}d_{\ell i})=0,
$$
$$
a_{\ell i}(d_{\ell j}-d_{ji})=0,
$$
$$
a_{ji}(d_{\ell j}d_{\ell i}-1)=0.
$$
If all six pairwise composites are simple, then every $a_{ji}\ne0$, so Step 2 gives
$$
d_{ji}=-1
$$
for every pair. The displayed Yang-Baxter relations are then automatic for every triple. Therefore
$$
S_p=6,
$$
and the first-stage maximizers are indexed freely by the six scalars
$$
(a_{21},a_{31},a_{41},a_{32},a_{42},a_{43})\in(k^\times)^6.
$$

Step 4: Identify the total composite as a Clifford algebra
For a first-stage maximizer, the total composite monad is tensoring by the $16$-dimensional algebra $A$ generated by $x_1,x_2,x_3,x_4$ with
$$
x_i^2=0,
$$
and
$$
x_jx_i+x_ix_j=a_{ji}
\qquad(i<j).
$$
Since $p$ is odd, this is the Clifford algebra of the symmetric bilinear form on the span of the $x_i$ whose Gram matrix is
$$
B=\frac12
\begin{pmatrix}
0&a_{21}&a_{31}&a_{41}\\
a_{21}&0&a_{32}&a_{42}\\
a_{31}&a_{32}&0&a_{43}\\
a_{41}&a_{42}&a_{43}&0
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Put
$$
x=a_{21}a_{43},
\qquad
y=a_{31}a_{42},
\qquad z=a_{41}a_{32}.
$$
A direct determinant expansion gives
$$
16\det B
=x^2+y^2+z^2-2xy-2xz-2yz.
$$

If $B$ is degenerate, choose $0\ne v$ in its radical. Then $v^2=0$ and $v$ anticommutes with every generator, so the nonzero ideal $vA$ is two-sided and satisfies
$$
(vA)^2=0.
$$
Hence $A$ is not simple.

If $B$ is nondegenerate, extend scalars to an algebraic closure $\bar k$. The nondegenerate $4$-dimensional form becomes a sum of two hyperbolic planes, whose Clifford algebra is
$$
M_2(\bar k)\otimes M_2(\bar k)\cong M_4(\bar k).
$$
Thus $A\otimes_k\bar k$ is simple. Any nonzero two-sided ideal of $A$ would remain a nonzero proper ideal after the faithfully flat extension $k\subset\bar k$, impossible. Therefore $A$ is simple exactly when
$$
\det B\ne0.
$$
So the second extremal value is
$$
T_p=1.
$$

Step 5: Count the total-simple first-stage maximizers
It remains to count the six-tuples in $(k^\times)^6$ for which
$$
\Delta(x,y,z)=x^2+y^2+z^2-2xy-2xz-2yz
$$
is nonzero.

For each fixed nonzero triple $(x,y,z)$, the equations
$$
a_{21}a_{43}=x,
\qquad
a_{31}a_{42}=y,
\qquad
a_{41}a_{32}=z
$$
have exactly $(p-1)^3$ solutions in the six edge parameters. Hence it suffices to count nonzero triples $(x,y,z)$ with $\Delta\ne0$.

Because $\Delta$ is homogeneous, scale by $z$ and put
$$
u=x/z,
\qquad v=y/z.
$$
Then
$$
\Delta(x,y,z)=0
$$
is equivalent to
$$
u^2+v^2+1-2uv-2u-2v=0.
$$
As a quadratic in $v$, this is
$$
v^2-2(u+1)v+(u-1)^2=0,
$$
whose discriminant is
$$
16u.
$$
For $u\in k^\times$, there are two roots exactly when $u$ is a square and no roots when $u$ is a nonsquare. There are $(p-1)/2$ nonzero squares. For $u=1$, one of the two roots is $v=0$, which is excluded; for every other square both roots are nonzero. Therefore the number of zeroes in $(k^\times)^2$ is
$$
2\cdot\frac{p-1}{2}-1=p-2.
$$
Hence the number of pairs $(u,v)\in(k^\times)^2$ with nonzero discriminant expression is
$$
(p-1)^2-(p-2)=p^2-3p+3.
$$
Restoring the arbitrary scale $z\in k^\times$, the number of nonzero triples $(x,y,z)$ with $\Delta\ne0$ is
$$
(p-1)(p^2-3p+3).
$$
Multiplying by the $(p-1)^3$ lifts of each triple gives
$$
K_p=(p-1)^4(p^2-3p+3).
$$

Final Answer: $\boxed{\left(6,1,(p-1)^4(p^2-3p+3)\right)}$
